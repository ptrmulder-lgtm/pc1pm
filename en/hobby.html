The hobby

Amateur radio in a nutshell

With a transmitter, an antenna and a piece of wire you can talk to someone on the other side of the world, without the internet or a phone network. Here is a short explanation of how that works.

01 · What is it?

Radio as a hobby

A radio amateur, or "ham", makes radio contacts with their own equipment, purely out of interest and for fun, never for money. To be allowed to transmit you pass an exam. After that you get your own call sign, unique worldwide. Mine is PC1PM. The prefix PA–PI tells listeners you are in the Netherlands.

What you do with it is up to you. Some love talking to faraway countries, others build their own antennas, experiment with satellites or take part in competitions, the so-called contests. A contact is called a QSO. As confirmation you exchange a QSL card, these days often digitally.

Radio amateurs are also there when it matters. When disasters knock out phone and internet networks, they can still provide emergency communications.

02 · History

More than a century of radio amateurs

  1. 1895
    Marconi goes wireless

    The Italian Guglielmo Marconi sends Morse signals through the air. By 1901 he reaches across the Atlantic Ocean.

  2. 1912
    Amateurs sent to "useless" shortwave

    The US government confines amateurs to wavelengths shorter than 200 metres, which were considered worthless. That turned out to be a mistake with big consequences.

  3. 1923
    First contact across the ocean

    Amateurs in France and the USA make contact with each other on shortwave for the first time. It proved that short waves can travel around the world.

  4. 1925
    The IARU is founded

    Radio amateurs from many countries found the International Amateur Radio Union in Paris, which still represents their interests today.

  5. 1945
    VERON is founded

    In the Netherlands, the Vereniging voor Experimenteel Radio Onderzoek in Nederland is founded: the national society of radio amateurs and organiser of the PACC contest.

  6. 2003
    Morse no longer required

    Internationally, Morse code is no longer a mandatory part of the exam. Still, CW remains hugely popular among many amateurs, including me.

  7. 2017
    FT8 changes the bands

    Nobel laureate Joe Taylor (K1JT) and Steve Franke (K9AN) introduce FT8, a digital mode that makes contacts possible with signals you can't even hear with your ears.

  8. Now
    Software and radio become one

    Modern transceivers are essentially computers (SDR). On screen you see the whole band as a waterfall of signals, and via the internet you instantly see who hears you where.

03 · The modes

How do you talk to the world?

A "mode" is the way information is put onto the radio signal. Some modes are great for ordinary conversations, others get through with very weak signals.

SSB

Single Side Band · voice

Simply talking into a microphone. SSB is an efficient form of AM: the redundant parts of the signal are left out, so all the power goes into your voice. It sounds a bit "tinny", but it goes a long way.

CW

Continuous Wave · Morse

The oldest and simplest signal: a tone switched on and off, in dots and dashes. A CW signal is very narrow, so it still gets through when voice has long disappeared into the noise.

RTTY

Radioteletype · text

The successor of the telex. Text is sent by rapidly switching between two tones. You hear a characteristic "chirping"; the computer turns it into readable text. Popular in contests.

FT8 / FT4

Digital · computer-driven

The computer exchanges short messages in fixed 15-second slots (FT4: 7.5 seconds): call sign, location and signal strength. It works with signals far below the noise level and now makes up a large share of the traffic on the bands.

SSTV

Slow Scan Television · pictures

A photo turned into sound. The image is sent line by line; after one or two minutes a complete picture appears on the other end. Even the ISS occasionally transmits SSTV images.

QSO

What do you actually exchange?

A basic contact is short: call signs, a signal report (for example "59") and often your name and location. In a contest it goes even faster, sometimes hundreds of contacts per hour.

04 · The HF bands

Shortwave: 3 to 30 MHz

HF ("High Frequency"), also called shortwave, is the range where you can reach the whole world without satellites or internet. Radio amateurs have a number of slices there, the bands, named after their wavelength in metres. Each band has its own character.

BandFrequencyCharacter
160 m1.8 MHzThe "top band". Only good for long distance at night, mainly in winter. Needs big antennas.
80 m3.5 MHzDomestic and neighbouring countries by day; all of Europe and beyond in the evening and at night.
40 m7 MHzA true all-rounder: Europe by day, other continents at night as well.
30 m10 MHzNarrow band, CW and digital only. No contests.
20 m14 MHzThe classic DX band: almost always open to somewhere far away.
17 m18 MHzQuiet and friendly, no contests. Often surprisingly good.
15 m21 MHzExcellent for long distance by day when solar activity is high.
12 m24.9 MHzSmall and quiet, no contests. Opens up when the sun is active.
10 m28 MHzFickle: sometimes dead for days, sometimes around the world with just a few watts.

Rule of thumb: the low bands (160–40 m) are best at night, the high bands (20–10 m) during the day.

05 · Propagation

Why it works (and sometimes doesn't)

Radio waves basically travel in straight lines, out into space. That they still make it around the earth is thanks to the ionosphere: layers of electrically charged air 60 to 400 km up, created by radiation from the sun. On shortwave this layer acts like a mirror. The signal bounces back to earth, reflects off the ground again, and in a few "hops" it can reach the other side of the world.

Day and night

By day the ionosphere is strongly charged: good for the high bands, but a lower layer (the D layer) soaks up the low bands. At night that damper disappears and 160, 80 and 40 m open up.

The solar cycle

The sun has a cycle of roughly 11 years. With many sunspots, the high bands open wide. Cycle 25 peaked around 2024–2025; activity will slowly decrease over the coming years.

The grey line

On the border between day and night, at sunrise and sunset, special openings often appear to places that are otherwise out of reach.

Sporadic E

In summer, "clouds" of charged air sometimes suddenly form at around 100 km altitude. Then 10 and 6 m suddenly open up to all of Europe, sometimes for just an hour.

Solar storms

An eruption on the sun can seriously disturb the ionosphere. The bands can then be practically dead for hours or even days.

Long and short path

A signal can take the shortest route, but sometimes it is heard better the other way around the globe: the "long path".

The numbers explained

The space weather widget shows a row of measurements. Here is how to read them. In short: a high solar flux is good, a high Kp index is bad.

SFI

Solar flux index

The amount of radio emission from the sun at 10.7 cm wavelength (2800 MHz), measured daily in Canada. More radiation means a stronger ionosphere, so higher frequencies are reflected.

< 80 low80–120 fair> 120 good

Higher is better. Above 150, 15, 12 and 10 m open wide during the day.

SSN

Sunspot number

The number of sunspots and sunspot groups on the sun. Spots are active regions; the more there are, the more active the sun. The number rises and falls with the roughly 11-year solar cycle.

0–50 quiet sun50–100> 100 active

Higher is better. Usually moves together with the solar flux.

Kp

Kp index · geomagnetic activity

How disturbed the earth's magnetic field is, on a scale of 0 to 9, calculated every three hours from measurements around the world. A disturbed field upsets the ionosphere: signals get weaker and start to "flutter", especially on paths over the poles.

0–2 quiet3–4 unsettled5–9 storm

Lower is better. At Kp 5 or more there is a geomagnetic storm, sometimes with aurora visible as far south as the Netherlands.

A

A index · daily average

The same idea as the Kp index, but as an average over a whole day on an ordinary (linear) scale from 0 to 400. Useful to see whether it was a quiet or a disturbed day.

< 8 quiet8–29 unsettled≥ 30 storm

Lower is better.

X-ray

X-rays · solar flares

X-ray emission from the sun, measured by GOES satellites. The classes A, B, C, M and X are each ten times stronger than the previous one. An M or X flare can wipe out HF on the daylight side of the earth within minutes: a radio blackout.

A–B quietC normalM–X flare

Lower is better. A blackout usually lasts minutes to a few hours.

km/s

Solar wind · speed

The sun constantly blows a stream of charged particles into space, normally at 300 to 500 km/s. A fast stream or an eruption (CME) puts pressure on the earth's magnetic field, often a sign that the Kp is about to rise.

< 450 normal450–600> 600 fast

Lower is calmer.

Bz / Bt

Magnetic field of the solar wind

Bt is the total strength of the magnetic field carried by the solar wind, in nanotesla (nT). Bz is its north–south direction. When Bz points south (negative), it "connects" with the earth's magnetic field and energy can pour in.

Bz positive0 to −10< −10 nT

A strongly negative Bz that lasts for hours is the classic run-up to a storm.

R · S · G

NOAA scales

Three scales from 0 to 5 for space weather, like the Beaufort scale for wind. R is for radio blackouts caused by flares, S for solar radiation storms (fast protons that mainly dampen polar paths) and G for geomagnetic storms (linked to the Kp).

0 none1–2 minor3–5 severe

All zeros is what you want.

06 · Unpredictable

You never know who you'll hear

That may well be the best part of this hobby. You can forecast conditions, but reality keeps surprising you. One evening the band is silent, the next morning a station from Japan, Brazil or a tiny Pacific island suddenly calls you back.

Sometimes a pile-up develops: hundreds of stations all trying to reach a rare country at once. Then it's a matter of patience, timing and a bit of luck.

That's why every contact with a new country is a little celebration. By now I have worked 214 DXCC entities, 200 of them confirmed. The cards on the wall are the best memories.

You switch on the radio without knowing where the world is open to today. That is exactly what makes it exciting.
